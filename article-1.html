Why Word Count Isn't the Whole Story

5 min read · Guides

A word count is easy to measure and easy to obsess over, which is exactly why it's a weak signal on its own. Two pieces of writing with the same word count can read completely differently — one tight and clear, the other padded and repetitive. The count tells you volume, not quality.

What a word count actually tells you

It's genuinely useful for two things: matching a hard requirement (a 500-word brief, a tweet's character limit) and rough pacing — knowing that 1,500 words will take a reader about seven or eight minutes. Beyond that, treating a target word count as a quality bar tends to produce padded writing, since it's easier to add filler than to cut well.

Better signals to check alongside it

  • Sentence length variation. Writing where every sentence runs the same length reads as monotonous, regardless of total word count.
  • Repeated words or phrases. If the same phrase shows up five times in three paragraphs, that's a padding signal a word counter can't catch.
  • Read-aloud test. Reading a paragraph out loud surfaces awkward, over-long sentences faster than staring at a character count does.
In practice: use the word counter to hit a hard limit or estimate reading time, then judge quality by reading the piece back, not by watching the number climb.

When word count does matter more directly

Some contexts genuinely need a precise count — academic submissions with strict limits, SEO meta descriptions, ad copy with character caps. In those cases, treat the counter as a hard constraint to satisfy, then step back and evaluate the writing on its own terms once you're within range.
